A used car that looks clean in photos and drives fine around the block can still be hiding a prior accident. Dealers and private sellers are not always forthcoming, and a car that was repaired well can pass a casual glance without trouble. The good news is that most accident history leaves traces you can actually find, if you know where to look and in what order to look. This guide walks through the visual inspection, the under-the-hood and interior checks, the test drive signs, and the paperwork step that together tell you whether a car's history matches what the seller is telling you.

Start with the body panel gaps

Every panel on a factory-assembled car — hood, doors, trunk, fenders — sits with a consistent, even gap relative to the panels around it. After a collision and repair, those gaps often come back slightly uneven because panels were unbolted, re-welded, or replaced without perfect factory alignment.

Open and close each door, the hood, and the trunk lid and watch how they move. They should swing smoothly and latch with the same solid feel on both sides of the car. Then stand at the front and back of the vehicle and sight down each body line — if a gap is noticeably wider on one side than the other, or a panel sits slightly recessed or proud compared to its neighbor, that is worth a closer look rather than an automatic deal-breaker.

Check the paint for mismatches

Paint is one of the easiest things to inspect and one of the hardest things to perfectly match in a repair shop. Walk the car in direct sunlight and compare the color and texture of each panel against the ones next to it. A repainted panel often reads slightly off in shade, has a different texture or sheen, or shows faint overspray on nearby trim, rubber seals, or glass edges.

Run your fingers lightly along a few panels. Factory paint feels smooth and uniform. Repainted sections sometimes feel slightly rougher, or show orange-peel texture that is more pronounced than the rest of the car. A paint thickness gauge, which costs under $30, removes the guesswork entirely — factory panels typically read in a tight, consistent range, while a repaired panel often reads noticeably thicker from extra primer and clear coat.

Look underneath and in the engine bay for repair evidence

The areas sellers are least likely to clean up for a sale are usually the most honest. Open the hood and look along the inner fender edges, the firewall, and the radiator support for ripples, fresh welds, or paint overspray that does not belong there. Factory welds are uniform spot welds in a straight pattern; repair welds are often uneven seam welds in places the factory would never weld.

If you can safely access the underside, check the frame rails and subframe for kinks, fresh undercoating applied in patches rather than evenly, or bolts and fasteners that look newer and shinier than everything around them. Clamp marks — small dented indentations where a frame-straightening machine gripped the metal — are one of the more reliable signs of a serious prior collision, since they have no other reasonable explanation.

Check the interior for airbag and mileage clues

A deployed airbag is expensive to replace correctly, and a dashboard that looks slightly uneven around the steering wheel, or a steering wheel cover that feels thicker or oddly textured compared to factory, can indicate an airbag was replaced. Pull back the edge of the headliner near the A-pillars to check for a replaced side curtain airbag, which sometimes leaves a visible seam.

Also compare interior wear to the odometer reading. Worn pedal rubber, a sagging driver's seat bolster, and a shiny, smooth steering wheel on a car reporting under 30,000 miles is a mismatch worth questioning, since it is far more consistent with genuinely higher use — or a rolled-back odometer — than with light driving.

Take it for a real test drive, not just a lap around the block

Drive on a straight, flat, empty road and briefly take your hands off the wheel (safely, and only if conditions allow) to see if the car pulls noticeably to one side. A consistent pull, combined with uneven tire wear, can point to frame or suspension damage from an impact rather than a simple alignment issue.

Listen for clunks over bumps, feel for vibration at highway speed, and notice if the car tracks straight when braking hard in a safe, empty area. Also pay attention to smell — a faint but persistent odor of fresh paint, sealant, or adhesive inside a car that is more than a year or two old is a sign recent bodywork happened, even if nothing looks visually off.

Pull a vehicle history report before you commit

Visual inspection catches a lot, but it cannot tell you about a salvage title, a flood history, or an insurance total-loss claim from a different state than the one the car is currently registered in. For that, you need a report pulled against the car's VIN (the 17-character vehicle identification number, usually visible on the dashboard through the windshield or on the driver's door jamb).

A report will flag things no amount of walking around the car can reveal: branded or salvage titles, reported accidents, airbag deployment events, odometer rollback flags, and whether the car has bounced between states to wash a bad title. It will not replace a mechanic's inspection, but it tells you whether it is even worth paying for one. You do not need to pay big-brand report prices to get this information — budget VIN lookup services like VinAlert check the same core accident, title, and odometer databases for a fraction of what the name-brand report sites charge, which makes it easy to run a check on every car you seriously consider instead of skipping the step to save money.

When to walk away

No single sign above automatically means a car is a bad buy — plenty of honest, well-repaired cars have one minor panel gap or a replaced bumper from a parking lot bump. What should make you cautious is a pattern: a mismatched panel and a vehicle history report that is silent about any accident, a seller who gets evasive or rushes you when you ask about past repairs, or a seller who refuses to let you get an independent mechanic's inspection before you buy. A good deal that cannot survive a VIN check and an hour with a trusted mechanic usually was not that good a deal to begin with.

Bottom line

Checking a used car for accident history is a short, repeatable routine: inspect panel gaps and paint in daylight, look under the hood and underneath for repair evidence, check the interior for airbag and mileage mismatches, pay attention on the test drive, and always pull a VIN-based history report before you hand over money. None of these steps take long individually, but together they catch the vast majority of hidden accident history before it becomes your problem instead of the seller's.
