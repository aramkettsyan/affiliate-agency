Why your dog still pulls even though you "trained" it

You've practiced, you've bought the treats, maybe you even watched a few videos, and your dog still drags you down the block the moment the front door opens. That is frustrating, but it is rarely a sign that your dog is stubborn or "dominant." Pulling is a learned habit, and habits survive training for a handful of very predictable reasons. The good news is that each one is fixable.

The core idea: dogs repeat what works. If pulling gets your dog closer to a smell, a squirrel, another dog, or simply moves the walk forward faster, then pulling is paying off, no matter how many practice sessions you've done at home.

Reason 1: Pulling still works some of the time

This is the most common culprit. Imagine a slot machine that pays out only occasionally. People keep pulling the lever precisely because the reward is unpredictable. Dogs are the same. If you hold firm for nine steps and give in on the tenth, because you're late, because there's a car coming, or because the dog is dragging you toward the mailbox, you've taught your dog that pulling is worth trying again.

  • Decide on one rule: a tight leash means we stop moving forward.
  • Apply it every walk, including the rushed ones. Short, boring, consistent walks teach more than long, inconsistent ones.
  • Reward the moment the leash goes slack, with a treat, praise, or permission to walk on to the thing the dog wanted.

Reason 2: The training only happened in easy places

A dog that walks nicely in the living room or the driveway may fall apart on a busy sidewalk. Distractions raise excitement, and an excited dog's training goes out the window. This doesn't mean the training failed; it means it hasn't been "generalized" to harder environments yet.

  • Practice in stages: inside, then the yard, then a quiet street, then a busier one.
  • If your dog ignores treats entirely, you are probably too close to the distraction. Add distance until the dog can eat and think again.
  • Keep sessions short, five to ten minutes of focused practice beats an hour of frustration.

Reason 3: Different people, different rules

If one family member lets the dog pull and another doesn't, the dog doesn't learn "no pulling"; it learns "pulling works with some people." Everyone who walks the dog should use the same cue, the same reward, and the same response to a tight leash.

Reason 4: Not enough exercise or sniffing before the walk

A dog that has been cooped up all day is wound like a spring. Ten minutes of fetch, tug, or a food puzzle before the walk can take the edge off and make it easier for your dog to think. Also remember that sniffing is how dogs read the world. A walk where your dog is never allowed to sniff is frustrating for them, and frustrated dogs pull harder. Build in "go sniff" breaks as a reward for walking nicely.

Reason 5: Fear or anxiety, not excitement

Not all pulling is eager pulling. Some dogs surge forward, or lunge, because they're scared of traffic, strangers, or other dogs and want to get away or get it over with. If your dog's body looks tense, the tail is tucked, or the pulling is paired with barking, lunging or freezing, you are dealing with a stress response, and treat-and-stop techniques alone won't resolve it. In that case, work at a distance where your dog stays calm, and consider help from a certified trainer or behavior professional.

Reason 6: Equipment is doing the wrong job

Retractable leashes teach dogs that pulling extends the leash, which is the opposite of what you want. A standard four-to-six-foot leash is much easier to train with. A front-clip harness can reduce your dog's ability to pull and make walks more manageable, but treat it as a management tool, not a cure; the dog still needs to learn what you do want. Avoid anything that causes pain or discomfort, which can worsen fear and reactivity.

A simple plan you can start tomorrow

  • Pick a quiet place and start with a short, 5-minute session.
  • Keep treats at your hip, on the side you want your dog to walk.
  • Reward every few steps while the leash is loose. Mark the good moment with a word like "yes."
  • When the leash goes tight, stop. Wait for it to slacken (a step back toward you counts), then reward and move on.
  • Gradually make the walk harder: longer stretches between rewards, then more distractions.
  • Track your wins for two weeks. Expect progress in steps, not a straight line.

When to get professional help

If your dog's pulling comes with sudden changes, such as new aggression, limping, coughing or gagging on the lead, or a dog who suddenly won't walk, see your vet first to rule out pain or a medical cause. If your dog lunges or reacts strongly toward other dogs or people, a qualified positive-reinforcement trainer or veterinary behaviorist can build a plan that's safe for everyone.

Where a structured program can help

Most pulling problems come down to consistency: a clear plan, practiced the same way by everyone, in gradually harder places.
