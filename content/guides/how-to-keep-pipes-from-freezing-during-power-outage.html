When the power fails in freezing weather, the furnace stops, and the plumbing becomes the part of the house most likely to leave you with a large repair bill. The usual advice ("let the taps drip and open the cabinets") is written for a house that still has heat. Once the heat is off completely, some of that advice works, some stops working, and if you're on a well it may not be possible at all.

The good news is that you have one very reliable option: a pipe with no water in it can't freeze and burst. Here is how to decide between protecting the pipes and draining them, and how to do each without electricity.

How long before pipes freeze without heat?

There is no single number. It depends on the outdoor temperature, the wind, how well the house is insulated, and above all where the pipes run. A temperature of around 20°F (about -7°C) outside is often quoted as the point where unprotected pipes in unheated spaces are at real risk, but a drafty crawl space can be a problem at milder temperatures, and a well-insulated house may stay safe much longer.

Published estimates for a house that has lost its heat vary, with some as short as six hours in below-freezing weather. Treat that as a reason to act early rather than as a deadline. A cheap thermometer placed in the coldest spot near your pipes, such as under the kitchen sink or in the basement near an outside wall, tells you more than any average. If that spot is falling toward 40°F and there is no sign of the power returning, start preparing to drain.

Know which pipes freeze first

  • Pipes in exterior walls, especially under kitchen and bathroom sinks.
  • Pipes in crawl spaces, attics, garages and unheated basements.
  • The supply line close to where it enters the house.
  • Outdoor faucets and the short section of pipe behind them, especially with a garden hose still attached.
  • Supply lines to an upstairs bathroom above a garage, or an outside-wall laundry.

Pipes running through interior walls in the middle of the house are usually the last to be affected.

The first hour: slow the heat loss

  • Find your main water shutoff and make sure it turns. You want to know this before a pipe splits, not after.
  • Disconnect garden hoses, and close and drain outdoor faucets if they have an indoor shutoff.
  • Close the garage door and any drafty openings you can safely close. Do not block vents that a fuel-burning appliance needs.
  • Open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls, as long as the room is warmer than the cabinet. Move cleaning chemicals out of reach of children and pets first.
  • Wrap exposed pipes in whatever you have: foam sleeves, towels, blankets or layers of newspaper held with tape. Keep wrapping away from flues, burners and pilot lights. It slows heat loss but adds no heat, so it buys time and nothing more.
  • Fill bottles and clean pots for drinking, and a bathtub for flushing, while you still have water.

Does dripping the faucets work in a power outage?

On city water, usually yes, for a while. Municipal supply often stays pressurized during a local outage, and the water coming from underground is above freezing. A thin, steady trickle from both the hot and cold sides of the faucets served by exposed pipes can help. Choose a faucet far from where the water enters the house so the flow passes through as much of the pipe as possible. Make sure the drain is clear and the trickle isn't freezing in it. Follow your water utility's instructions, since some ask customers not to run taps during a widespread emergency.

On a private well, usually no. Without electricity the pump stops. You have only whatever is left in the pressure tank, and dripping it away does little. On a well, plan to drain sooner.

Dripping also isn't enough if the house itself is going well below freezing for a long time. Moving water buys time; it doesn't replace heat.

When to drain the pipes

Drain the system if the house is heading toward freezing and any of these is true: the outage is expected to last through a very cold night or longer, you are leaving the house, you have a well pump with no power, or your pipes have frozen before. It costs nothing except some inconvenience.

How to drain your pipes without power

  • Make the water heater safe first. For an electric heater, switch its breaker off so it can't come on with an empty tank when the power returns. For a gas heater, follow the instructions on its label, normally turning the control to "pilot" or "off."
  • Shut off the main water valve. On a well, switch off the pump breaker as well.
  • Open every faucet, hot and cold, starting at the top of the house. Include showers and the laundry sink. Open faucets let air in so the water can run out.
  • Open the lowest faucet or drain valve in the house, often in the basement or near the main shutoff, and catch the water in a bucket if needed.
  • Flush the toilets to empty the tanks and sponge out what remains. Some water will stay in the bowl.
  • Leave the faucets open. If a little water is left behind, it has room to expand.

The water heater's tank holds a lot of heat and is generally one of the slower things to freeze, but in a long outage in a frigid house it needs draining too. The water can be scalding, so use a hose to a safe drain and take your time.

Sink, tub and shower traps hold water by design. In a prolonged freeze, non-toxic antifreeze labeled for plumbing (often sold as RV antifreeze) can be poured into them. Never use automotive antifreeze; it is poisonous to people and pets.

Systems you should not drain on a guess

Some equipment needs specific steps. Read the manual or call a professional for hot-water (hydronic) heating boilers and radiators, fire sprinkler systems, tankless water heaters, water softeners and filters, and dishwashers or washing machines, which can hold water in pumps and hoses. Tankless units deserve special attention, because the built-in freeze protection on many of them needs electricity.

If a pipe has already frozen

The clue is a faucet that gives a trickle or nothing while others work. Shut off the main water valve, then open the faucet so melting water can escape. Warm the pipe gently, starting from the faucet end and working back toward the frozen section, using towels soaked in warm water if there's no power. Expect it to be slow.

Never use a torch, a candle or any open flame, which can start a fire inside the wall. Don't run a grill, camp stove or generator indoors or in the garage to warm the space: carbon monoxide can kill quickly, and you can't see or smell it. If you can't find the frozen section, can't reach it, or see a bulge or crack, leave the water off and call a licensed plumber.

When the power comes back

Let the house warm up before you refill anything. Close the faucets except one on each floor, open the main valve slowly, and walk the house looking and listening for leaks. Let the open faucets run until they stop spitting air. Only turn the water heater back on when the tank is full and a hot tap runs in a steady stream. If any pipe leaks, shut the main again straight away.

Decide before the next storm

Almost everything here is easier if it's written down in advance: where the main shutoff is, which pipes are at risk, who turns off the heater and at what temperature you stop dripping and drain. A one-page sheet taped near the shutoff is enough. If you'd like that thinking extended to food, water and power as well, a structured household plan organized around short, medium and longer outages can help you fill the gaps one step at a time.
