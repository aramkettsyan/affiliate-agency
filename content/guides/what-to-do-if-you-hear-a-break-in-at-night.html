The sound that jolts you awake — a window sliding, a floorboard creaking somewhere it shouldn't, a door handle turning — lasts maybe two seconds. What you do in the next thirty matters more than almost anything else you'll do that night. Most people have never actually rehearsed this moment, so when it happens for real, adrenaline takes over and instinct fills the gap where a plan should be. That instinct is usually wrong. Here's what actually works, based on how home invasions unfold and what security professionals and law enforcement consistently recommend.

First, don't move — listen

Your first job is not to act, it's to confirm. Houses make noise on their own: settling wood, an ice maker, a pet knocking something over, wind against a loose gutter. Before you do anything else, lie still for a few seconds and listen for a second sound. A single noise that doesn't repeat is often nothing. Footsteps, a second door, muffled voices, or the sound repeating from a different location means you should treat it as real and move to the next step immediately.

This pause costs almost no time, but it matters because the wrong first move — flipping on lights, shouting into the house, or walking toward the noise to investigate — can turn an ambiguous situation into a dangerous confrontation you didn't need to have.

Get behind a locked door and call for help

Once you believe someone is in the house, your priority is distance and a barrier between you and them, not confrontation. If you're in a bedroom, close and lock the door immediately. A hollow-core interior door with a cheap privacy lock won't stop a determined intruder for long, but it buys you seconds, and seconds are what you need to call 911.

  • Call or text 911 from your cell phone — a landline in another room isn't worth the walk.
  • Keep your voice low. Give the dispatcher your address first, in case the call drops, then describe what you're hearing.
  • Stay on the line if the dispatcher allows it, but keep it quiet enough that it doesn't reveal your location.
  • If you have kids or others in the house, and it's safe to reach them without leaving your secured room, bring them in with you. If it isn't safe, tell the dispatcher exactly where they are so responding officers know.

Resist the urge to go check on noises elsewhere in the house, wake up and gather everyone in one open hallway, or go looking for the intruder to scare them off. Moving through a house in the dark toward an unknown threat is how encounters escalate. Let a locked door and a phone call do the work.

Why turning on the lights can work against you

It feels natural to flip on every light in the house — light feels like control. But bright lights do two things you don't want: they ruin your night vision, which you'll need if the situation continues, and they can silhouette you clearly if the intruder is positioned to see into the room, while doing little to stop someone who's already inside. Exterior lights and motion-activated floodlights are excellent as prevention, aimed at a would-be intruder before they get in. Once someone is already inside, interior lights mostly help them, not you.

The same logic applies to shouting a challenge like "I know you're in here" or "I have a gun." It might work as a bluff that sends someone running, but it also tells the intruder exactly where you are in a home they may not have fully mapped out yet. If you have a plan to actively defend the room you're in, prepare for that quietly rather than announcing it.

Have a plan before you ever need one

Everything above works far better when it isn't improvised at 2 a.m. for the first time. A few minutes of planning, done in daylight when you're calm, changes how this actually plays out:

  • Pick a safe room. Usually the primary bedroom — it should have a door that locks, a phone or charger kept there permanently, and enough space for family members to gather if needed.
  • Walk the path in the dark. Practice getting from where you sleep to the safe room without turning on a light, so you're not relearning your own hallway layout under stress.
  • Agree on a signal word. Something you can say to family members that means "go to the safe room now" without alarming an intruder if overheard.
  • Set a rally point outside. A specific spot — a neighbor's porch, the mailbox, a tree at the property line — where everyone regroups if the house needs to be evacuated instead of sheltered in place.
  • Keep phones charged and reachable. A phone across the room at 20% battery is a phone you might not have when you need it.

It's also worth thinking honestly about the seconds before any of this — the entry points that make a break-in possible in the first place. Most residential burglaries are opportunistic: an unlocked sliding door, a window left cracked, a side door out of view from the street. A locked bedroom door and a fast 911 call are your response plan; reinforced entry points, better exterior lighting, and a realistic read on your home's actual weak spots are what reduce how often you ever need that plan at all.

After the immediate danger has passed

If police confirm the house is clear, don't rush to "fix" anything before they've documented the scene — a forced door, a broken window, or footprints outside can matter for both the police report and any insurance claim. Take photos once you're told it's safe. If anything was disturbed or taken, write down what you remember while it's fresh, even before the full inventory is done.

It's also normal to feel shaky, unable to sleep, or on edge for days afterward, even if nothing worse happened. That reaction is a normal response to a real threat, not an overreaction — give yourself the same grace you'd give anyone else who went through it.

None of this requires a background in tactics or self-defense to get right — it requires a plan you've actually thought through before the moment arrives, not during it.
