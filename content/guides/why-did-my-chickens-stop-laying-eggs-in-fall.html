The Short Answer: It's Usually Molting, Shorter Days, or Both

If the nest boxes went quiet sometime between September and November, the most likely explanation is the calendar, not a problem with your flock. Hens lay in response to day length, and as the days shorten, most of them slow down or stop. Fall is also when many older hens molt, dropping their feathers and putting the energy that would have gone into eggs into growing new ones. Both are normal, and both are temporary.

Still, hidden nests, egg-eating, parasites, and illness can all look like a fall slowdown. Here's how to tell which you're dealing with, what helps, and when to get a sick bird looked at.

Check the Obvious First: Are You Sure They've Stopped?

Before changing anything, spend a few days ruling out eggs that are being laid but not reaching you:

  • Hidden nests. Free-range hens love a new spot. Check under shrubs, behind the coop, in the woodpile, and in any quiet corner of the shed.
  • Egg-eating hens. Look for shell fragments, yolk on beaks, or smears in the nest box. Collecting eggs earlier and more often usually reveals this quickly.
  • Thieves. Rats, snakes, skunks, opossums, jays, and crows all take eggs, often leaving little behind. New gaps in the coop or eggs vanishing overnight are the clues to look for.
  • Broodiness. A hen who sits tight in the nest, puffs up, and growls at you isn't laying while she's broody, and she may keep other hens out of that box too.

Is It Molting? What to Look For

Molting is hard to miss once you know the signs. Feathers turn up all over the coop floor, and the hen starts to look scruffy, sometimes with bare patches on her neck, back, or tail. When the new feathers come in, you'll see pin feathers: short, spiky shafts poking through the skin. These are tender, so she may not want to be handled.

A molt commonly lasts somewhere around 8 to 16 weeks, and it varies a lot from bird to bird. Some hens lose feathers gradually; others drop nearly everything in a couple of weeks. Most lay little or nothing during this period.

Age matters here. Hens usually go through their first full molt in their second fall, at around 15 to 18 months old. A spring-hatched pullet often skips molting in her first autumn. So if your one-year-old hens look terrible and your young pullets look perfect, that's the expected pattern, not a warning sign.

Is It the Light? The Part Most People Don't Expect

People tend to blame the cold, but day length is the bigger trigger. Light entering through a hen's eyes and skull stimulates the hormones that drive egg production. Extension guidance commonly puts the target at about 14 to 16 hours of light per day for steady laying, and production tends to fall off as natural daylight drops well below that.

In much of the northern United States, day length falls under 12 hours around the autumn equinox in late September and keeps shrinking until late December. That's why a flock with no feathers on the floor can still go quiet in November. They aren't molting; they just don't have enough daylight to keep laying.

Check Your Hens: A Two-Minute Hands-On Test

If you're not sure whether a particular hen is laying, her body will usually tell you. Pick her up calmly at dusk, when she's on the roost and easy to catch, then check these:

  • Comb and wattles. Bright, full, and waxy suggests she's in lay. Pale and a bit shrunken suggests she isn't.
  • Vent. Moist, oval, and pale in a laying hen; smaller, tighter, and drier in one who's taken a break.
  • Pelvic bones. The two points on either side of the vent are often around two or three finger-widths apart in a laying hen, and closer together in one who isn't.

None of these is perfect, but together they help separate "she's resting" from "she's laying somewhere I haven't found."

Other Causes Worth Ruling Out

If it isn't molt or daylight, or if the drop is sudden and doesn't fit the season, work through these:

  • Feed. Laying hens need a complete layer feed. Too many kitchen scraps or too much scratch dilutes their protein and calcium. Most keepers treat treats as a small add-on, not a meal.
  • Water. A hen that can't drink won't lay. In cold snaps, a frozen waterer can knock production down quickly, so check it twice a day when temperatures drop.
  • Stress. A predator scare, new birds added to the flock, a move to a new coop, or a bully at the feeder can all interrupt laying for a while.
  • Parasites. Part the feathers around the vent and look for mites or lice. Check at night with a flashlight for roost mites, which hide in cracks during the day.
  • Age. Production generally declines as hens get older, often noticeably after the first couple of laying seasons.

When to Call a Vet

A seasonal break should leave you with a hen who looks tired but acts normally: eating, drinking, scratching around, and heading up to roost with the others. Please contact a vet who sees poultry if you notice a hen hunched or sitting with her tail down, straining with nothing coming out, a swollen abdomen, labored breathing, discharge from the eyes or nose, diarrhea that won't clear, or isolation from the flock. A hen who's egg-bound can go downhill quickly, so that one shouldn't wait. This article is general information, not veterinary advice.

What Actually Helps During a Molt

You can't rush a molt much, but you can make it easier:

  • Raise the protein. Feathers are mostly protein, so many keepers switch to a higher-protein feed while the flock molts. Keep calcium available on the side, such as oyster shell in a separate dish, so any hens still laying can take what they need.
  • Handle less. Pin feathers are sensitive, so skip the cuddling until the new feathers open.
  • Watch for bullying. Bare, newly feathered birds can draw pecking. Add a second feeder and waterer so lower-ranking hens can eat in peace.

Should You Add Light to the Coop in Winter?

This is a real choice, not a must-do. Plenty of keepers let their hens rest through winter and accept fewer eggs. If you'd like winter eggs, a timer-controlled light can maintain production:

  • Aim for about 14 total hours of light, and don't go past 16.
  • Add the light in the morning, before dawn, not in the evening. That way the natural dusk still gets the birds onto the roost, instead of the light switching off and leaving them stranded in the dark.
  • Bring it on gradually, adding a little time each week rather than jumping straight to the full schedule.
  • Wait until molting birds have grown their new feathers before pushing them back into production.
  • Take fire safety seriously. Coops are full of dust and dry bedding. Use a fixture rated for the environment, secure it so it can't fall, and keep it well away from bedding. A plain LED is enough; you don't need a heat lamp to provide light.

A common middle ground is to let hens finish their molt naturally, then decide based on how much you want eggs this winter.

When Should Eggs Come Back?

Hens under natural light usually pick back up as the days lengthen after the winter solstice, with production building through late winter into spring. Molting hens typically return once the new feathers are in, provided there's enough daylight. If a young hen still isn't laying in spring when the rest of the flock is, look harder at feed, parasites, hidden nests, and her health.
