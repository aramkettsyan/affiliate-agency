Your dog is fine all day — eats normally, plays, acts totally themselves — and then first thing in the morning, before breakfast, they throw up a small puddle of yellow or foamy liquid. No food in it, nothing scary-looking, just bile. It happens again a few days later, always around the same time, always on an empty stomach. If this sounds familiar, there's a good chance your dog has what veterinarians call bilious vomiting syndrome, and in most cases it's one of the more manageable things a dog owner will deal with — once you understand what's actually happening.

What's actually going on in your dog's stomach

Bilious vomiting syndrome (sometimes called reflux gastritis) happens when bile — a digestive fluid made by the liver and stored in the gallbladder to help break down fat — backs up from the small intestine into an empty stomach. Bile isn't meant to sit in the stomach; it's meant to be in the intestine, mixing with food that's already been partially digested. When the stomach has been empty for many hours, usually overnight, that bile pools and starts irritating the stomach lining. The result is exactly what you see: your dog vomits a small amount of yellow, sometimes greenish or foamy liquid, generally with no food in it, most often first thing in the morning or occasionally late at night before a very early breakfast.

The key detail that separates this from a lot of other vomiting causes is timing. Bilious vomiting syndrome shows up specifically on an empty stomach, after the longest gap between meals in a dog's day, and then resolves as soon as the dog eats. A dog with this pattern usually acts completely normal otherwise — good appetite once the vomiting passes, normal energy, normal stools. That combination (empty-stomach timing plus an otherwise healthy dog) is the pattern vets look for.

Why it happens

The short version is that some dogs' stomachs are more sensitive to this backup than others, and a few things make it more likely:

  • A long overnight fasting window. The longer the gap between the last meal of the day and the first meal of the next, the more time bile has to accumulate and irritate an empty stomach.
  • Individual sensitivity. Some dogs simply have a stomach lining that reacts more to bile exposure than others, the same way some people get heartburn easily and others never do.
  • Motility issues. In some dogs, the valve between the stomach and small intestine (the pylorus) doesn't fully prevent backflow, which lets bile drift upstream more easily than it should.
  • Diet composition. Very low-fiber, low-residue diets can be digested and pass through faster, leaving the stomach empty for longer stretches between meals, which gives bile more opportunity to sit and irritate.
  • Stress or a recently upset stomach. A stomach already inflamed from something else (a dietary indiscretion, a stressful event) can be more reactive to bile exposure for a while afterward.

How vets typically confirm it

There's no single blood test or scan that says "yes, this is bilious vomiting syndrome." It's what's called a diagnosis of exclusion: your vet rules out the more serious possibilities first — pancreatitis, kidney or liver disease, a partial intestinal blockage, parasites, inflammatory bowel disease — usually with a physical exam and basic bloodwork, sometimes imaging if anything looks off. If your dog is otherwise healthy, eating and drinking normally, keeping food down once they do eat, and the vomiting fits that consistent empty-stomach pattern, bilious vomiting syndrome becomes the most likely explanation.

This is a case where seeing your veterinarian first really matters — not because the condition itself is usually dangerous, but because the symptoms overlap with things that can be serious. Don't self-diagnose from a search engine and skip the vet visit, especially the first time this happens.

When to see the vet promptly (don't wait this one out)

  • Vomiting happens more than once or twice a week, or the pattern is changing
  • There's blood in the vomit, or the vomit looks like coffee grounds
  • Your dog is also lethargic, refusing food, or losing weight
  • Diarrhea, a swollen or painful abdomen, or repeated unproductive retching is also happening
  • Vomiting happens at times other than the classic empty-stomach window, or with food in it
  • This is the first time it's happened and you haven't had it checked out yet

Any of those point toward something that needs a vet's evaluation rather than a home fix, and in a large, deep-chested breed, a swollen belly with unproductive retching in particular needs emergency care.

What actually helps once bilious vomiting syndrome is confirmed

The good news is that this specific condition usually responds well to a straightforward fix: shorten the overnight fasting window so the stomach isn't empty for as long. That's the first thing most vets recommend, and it's often enough on its own.

  • Add a late-night snack. A small meal or handful of kibble right before bed, on top of the normal dinner, closes the long overnight gap that lets bile accumulate.
  • Split the day into smaller, more frequent meals. Three or four smaller meals spread through the day, instead of one or two large ones, keeps the stomach from sitting empty for extended stretches at any point.
  • Move breakfast earlier if that's not practical. If a bedtime snack doesn't fit your routine, feeding first thing when you wake up (before the vomiting episode would typically happen) can help, though it's usually less effective than closing the overnight gap directly.
  • Reassess fat and fiber in the diet. Diets that move through the gut very quickly can leave the stomach empty sooner than expected; a vet or a food-focused approach can help you evaluate whether your dog's current food is part of the pattern.
  • Medication, if lifestyle changes aren't enough. For dogs where feeding adjustments alone don't fully resolve it, vets sometimes prescribe an acid reducer or a medication that helps the stomach empty more efficiently. This is a vet decision, not something to start on your own.

What to watch and track

Before and after making changes, it helps to keep a simple log: the time of each vomiting episode, what (if anything) was in it, when your dog last ate, and how your dog seemed afterward. That pattern is exactly what a vet needs to confirm the diagnosis in the first place, and it's the clearest way to tell whether a late-night snack or smaller, more frequent meals are actually working for your specific dog. Most dogs on this kind of adjusted schedule see the vomiting drop off noticeably within one to two weeks.

The bottom line

A dog that reliably throws up a small amount of yellow bile on an empty stomach, and is otherwise completely healthy, most likely has bilious vomiting syndrome — a real, well-documented, and generally manageable condition once your vet has ruled out anything more serious. The fix is usually about timing and structure around meals rather than a dramatic diet overhaul: closing the overnight gap, spacing meals out more evenly, and paying attention to what and when your dog is actually eating.
