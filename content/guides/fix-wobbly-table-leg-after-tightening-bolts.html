Why Tightening the Bolts Didn't Fix It

You flip the table over, grab a wrench, snug up every bolt you can find, flip it back — and it still rocks the second you set a glass down. This is one of the most common furniture-repair frustrations, and it happens because a wobble almost never has just one cause. Tightening hardware fixes exactly one possible problem: loose fasteners. If the real issue is somewhere else, tightening bolts that were already reasonably snug does nothing, and you end up convinced the table is simply broken.

Before you replace anything, it helps to actually diagnose which of the four usual suspects is causing your specific wobble. Each one has a distinct fix, and guessing wrong just wastes an afternoon.

Suspect #1: The Floor, Not the Table

This is the single most overlooked cause. A table can be structurally perfect and still wobble because the floor under it isn't flat — old hardwood, uneven tile grout lines, and slightly warped subfloors are all common culprits.

The test takes thirty seconds: push the table to a different spot in the room, or better yet, into a completely different room with a different floor. If the wobble disappears or changes which corner rocks, your floor is the problem, not the table. In that case, self-adhesive felt-and-rubber leveling pads or a set of adjustable furniture glides (screwed or tapped into the bottom of each leg) solve it permanently without touching the joinery at all.

Suspect #2: Legs of Slightly Different Lengths

Wood tables in particular can develop unequal leg lengths over time from uneven wear, moisture absorption, or a leg that was cut a hair short at the factory. A difference as small as 1/8 inch is enough to produce a very noticeable wobble on a hard floor.

To check, flip the table upside down on the flattest surface you have (a kitchen counter or a garage floor works better than carpet) and measure each leg from where it meets the apron or tabletop down to the bottom of the foot. If one leg is shorter, you have two real options: trim the longer legs down to match using a hand saw and a simple jig, or shim the short leg with stacked felt pads. Shimming is faster; trimming looks better and holds up longer.

Suspect #3: Stripped or Undersized Fastener Holes

This is why "tightening" sometimes makes no difference at all. Wood screws and bolts grip by biting into wood fiber or threading into a metal insert. If a screw has been removed and reinserted several times, or was overtightened at some point, the fibers around the hole can strip out completely — at that point the screw just spins freely in an oversized hole no matter how hard you crank it.

You can usually feel this: a bolt or screw that turns smoothly with almost no resistance right up until it stops is a strong sign of a stripped hole. The fix is straightforward. Back the fastener out, fill the stripped hole with wood glue and toothpicks or thin wood shims (let it cure fully, usually 24 hours), then redrill a pilot hole and reinsert the original screw. For a more permanent repair on a joint you expect to stress repeatedly, swap in a slightly larger diameter screw or a threaded insert instead of just refilling the old hole.

Suspect #4: A Failing Glue Joint Under the Apron

Bolts and screws hold the big structural connections, but a lot of table wobble actually comes from a corner brace or apron joint where the original glue bond has failed — often invisibly, since the piece can still look fully assembled while the joint itself has separated at a microscopic level.

Grip the tabletop with both hands and gently try to rock each corner independently while a helper watches the underside for any visible movement or gap opening and closing at a joint. If you find one, the fastest fix is working fresh wood glue into the gap with a thin applicator tip, clamping it snugly, and leaving it fully undisturbed for 24 hours. If that same corner has failed more than once, add a mechanical corner brace (an L-shaped or triangular metal bracket, screwed into both the apron and the leg) alongside the glue — it's a five-minute upgrade that outlasts a glue-only repair by years.

A Quick Diagnostic Order That Saves Time

  • Move the table to another spot first — if the wobble follows the table rather than the room, it's not the floor.
  • Flip it over and measure all four legs against each other before touching any tools.
  • Check each fastener by feel — does it tighten with resistance, or spin loosely?
  • Rock each corner by hand and watch the joints underneath for movement.
  • Fix in that order: floor, then legs, then fasteners, then glue joints. Fixing a stripped screw hole on a table that's actually sitting on an uneven floor won't solve anything.

When It's Time to Stop Repairing and Start Building

Most wobbles are fixable with the steps above, especially on a table that's otherwise solid. But if you're working through this checklist on a piece that's also cracking at the veneer, missing hardware entirely, or was cheaply built to begin with, you're often better off putting that repair time toward something that will actually last — a table, bench, or set of shelves you build yourself, sized and joined the way you want it.
