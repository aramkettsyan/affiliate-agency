You have been doing box jumps, skipping rope and hopping up stairs for weeks, yet the number on your vertical jump test has not moved. It is a frustrating plateau, and it is very common. The good news is that a stalled vertical jump usually comes down to a handful of fixable problems rather than a lack of talent. This guide walks through the most likely causes and how to check each one.

First, make sure you are measuring the same way every time

Before changing your training, rule out measurement noise. A jump measured on a different surface, with a different shoe, or with a slightly different standing reach can easily swing by an inch or two.

  • Test on the same surface, at the same time of day, after the same warm-up.
  • Use the same method each time: a wall-chalk test, a jump mat, or a phone app that you trust and always use the same way.
  • Take your best of three attempts, with a full minute of rest between them.
  • Test every two to three weeks, not every day. Daily testing just measures fatigue.

Cause 1: Too much plyometric work, not enough recovery

Plyometrics are high-impact and demanding on the nervous system and tendons. More is not better. If you are doing jump exercises five or six days a week, you may be arriving at every session tired, and tired jumps do not train explosive power well.

  • Keep high-intensity jump sessions to two or three per week, with at least a day between them.
  • Count quality contacts (foot landings), not minutes. Beginners often do well with roughly 60 to 100 contacts per session, building up gradually.
  • Stop a set when your jumps get visibly lower or sloppier. The goal is maximal effort on every rep.
  • Sleep and food matter. Chronic under-eating or short sleep will blunt power output.

Cause 2: No progression

Your body adapts to whatever you repeat. If you have done the same pogo hops and box jumps for two months, there is little new stimulus left. Progress by making exercises harder in a controlled way:

  • Move from low-intensity jumps (pogos, jump rope) to medium (squat jumps, box jumps) and then to higher-intensity work (depth jumps, single-leg bounds) only when you can land softly and quietly.
  • Increase box height, add a pause before the jump, or switch to single-leg versions.
  • Follow a block of at least 8 to 12 weeks. Research reviews on plyometric training generally find that longer programs with enough total sessions tend to produce larger gains than a few weeks of casual jumping.

Cause 3: Your legs are not strong enough yet

Jump height is partly a strength problem. If your squat, lunge and hip hinge strength has not improved in a long time, jump training alone may stop working. Many coaches pair plyometrics with basic strength work: squats or leg press, Romanian deadlifts, split squats and calf raises, two to three times per week. Build strength with good form and progressive loading, and do not skip warm-ups.

Cause 4: You already jump a lot in your sport

There is some research suggesting that athletes who already perform many maximal jumps in practice, such as volleyball players, may see smaller gains from extra plyometrics. If that is you, adding more jumping on top of a heavy practice schedule may simply add fatigue. In that case, focus on strength, recovery and technique rather than more volume.

Cause 5: Technique leaks

Small mechanical problems can cost real height:

  • Arm swing: a powerful, well-timed arm drive adds noticeable height. Swing arms back and then up hard as you leave the ground.
  • Countermovement depth: dropping too deep wastes time and elastic energy; too shallow limits force. Aim for a quick dip to a comfortable quarter to half squat.
  • Ground contact time: in reactive jumps, think "hot ground". Spend as little time on the floor as possible.
  • Full extension: finish tall through the hips, knees and ankles before you land.

Film yourself from the side on your phone. Seeing your own jump often reveals issues immediately.

Cause 6: Body composition and mobility

Extra body weight that is not muscle works against your jump, and stiff ankles or tight hips can limit how deep and fast you can load. A few minutes of ankle and hip mobility before jumping is a cheap fix.

A simple 8-week reset plan

  • Weeks 1 to 2: two plyometric sessions per week at low-to-medium intensity, two strength sessions, and a test at the end of week 2.
  • Weeks 3 to 5: raise intensity (higher boxes, single-leg work), keep contacts moderate, and keep strength training steady.
  • Weeks 6 to 7: add a few depth-jump or bounding sets only if your landings are controlled.
  • Week 8: lighter week, then a fresh test.

When to see a professional

Sharp knee, Achilles or shin pain during or after jumping is not something to push through. Jump training puts heavy load on tendons and joints, so if you have existing injuries, are returning from one, or are a young athlete still growing, check with a doctor, physiotherapist or qualified coach before ramping up intensity.
