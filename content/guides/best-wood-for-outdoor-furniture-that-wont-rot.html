You spend a weekend building a bench or planter box, and two summers later the legs are soft, gray, and crumbling at the bottom. The problem usually is not your joinery. It is the wood choice and how the piece meets the ground and the weather. This guide covers which woods actually hold up outside, which cheap options are fine if you finish them properly, and the design habits that matter more than the species.

Why outdoor wood rots in the first place

Wood rots when fungi get three things at once: moisture, oxygen, and a mild temperature. Remove any one and rot stops. Since you cannot remove oxygen or control the weather, the practical goal is to keep the wood from staying wet. Rain on a surface that dries within a few hours is rarely a problem. Water that gets trapped in a joint, soaks up through a leg end, or sits in a flat spot for days is what starts decay.

That is why the same board can last twenty years as a fence rail and rot in three as a table leg resting on a damp patio.

Naturally rot-resistant woods

Some species contain natural oils and tannins that fungi dislike. These come from the heartwood, the darker center of the tree. Sapwood from the same species is not nearly as durable, so look for boards that are mostly heartwood.

  • Western red cedar: lightweight, easy to cut, and widely available. Good for benches, planters, and trellises. It is soft, so it dents.
  • Redwood (heartwood grades): similar durability to cedar and a classic choice where it is sold locally.
  • White oak: dense and strong, with closed pores that resist water. Heavier and harder to work, but excellent for tables and chairs.
  • Black locust: one of the most durable domestic woods, often used for posts. Very hard, so pre-drill everything.
  • Teak and ipe: extremely durable imports. They cost far more, and it is worth checking that the supplier sources them responsibly.
  • Cypress: a solid regional option in the southern United States.

Budget options that still work

Not every project justifies cedar prices. Pressure-treated pine is the standard budget choice for anything touching the ground, such as raised bed frames, deck posts, and the legs of heavy furniture. Modern treated lumber is sold in grades for above-ground use and ground-contact use, so read the tag at the lumber yard and buy the ground-contact grade for anything that sits in soil or on concrete.

If you are building a vegetable planter, check the manufacturer's guidance on the specific treatment used, and consider lining the inside with plastic sheeting so soil does not sit directly against the wood.

Ordinary construction pine or fir can survive outside if it is sealed on every face, including the end grain, and kept off the ground. Expect to refinish it every year or two.

Species matters less than these design habits

  • Keep end grain off the ground. End grain soaks up water like a bundle of straws. Put metal feet, plastic glides, or a small concrete paver under every leg.
  • Leave small gaps in the slats. About 1/8 to 1/4 inch between seat and tabletop boards lets water drain and air move.
  • Avoid flat, water-catching joints. Slightly bevel the top of horizontal rails, and avoid pockets or recesses where puddles form.
  • Use the right fasteners. Standard steel screws corrode and stain the wood black. Use stainless steel or hot-dip galvanized hardware, and be careful because some treated lumber corrodes plain galvanized fasteners faster.
  • Use exterior glue. Choose a waterproof exterior wood glue rated for outdoor use, not basic interior yellow glue.
  • Seal the end grain twice. Brush an extra coat of finish onto every cut end before assembly.

Choosing a finish

Finishes fall into two broad groups. Penetrating oils and stains soak in, are easy to reapply, and wear away gradually without peeling. Film-forming finishes like exterior paint or spar varnish protect well at first but can crack and trap moisture underneath once the film fails. For most hobby projects, a penetrating exterior oil or semi-transparent stain is the easier long-term choice because you can recoat without stripping.

Whatever you choose, plan on annual or biannual maintenance. A quick wash and a fresh coat each spring costs far less than rebuilding the piece.

A quick checklist before you buy lumber

  • Will any part touch soil or concrete? If yes, use ground-contact treated wood or a heartwood of a durable species.
  • Is the piece in full sun, shade, or under cover? Constant shade and damp are harder on wood than dry sun.
  • How much maintenance will you really do? If the answer is none, spend more on the lumber.
  • Can you raise the legs off the surface with hardware?

When to repair instead of replace

Press a screwdriver into suspect areas. If it sinks in easily or the wood crumbles, the section is rotten and should be cut out. Small spots in non-structural pieces can be treated with a wood hardener and filler, but any rot in a load-bearing leg or seat rail is a safety issue and the part should be replaced. If the furniture is something people sit on, check the joints each season.
