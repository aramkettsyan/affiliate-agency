A parrot that screams for ten minutes at sunrise is being a parrot. A parrot that screams for hours, every day, until your ears ring and the neighbors start noticing is telling you something. The good news: screaming is almost always a behavior with a cause, and most causes can be fixed without punishment, shouting, or giving the bird away.

First: Is It Normal Screaming or a Problem?

Wild parrots live in flocks and use loud contact calls to keep track of each other, especially around dawn and dusk. Your bird treats your household as its flock, so some noise at those times is natural and will never fully disappear. What you are trying to reduce is the constant, repetitive, ear-splitting kind that happens throughout the day or demands a response.

Species matter too. Conures, cockatoos, macaws and Amazons are naturally louder than budgies or cockatiels. A realistic goal is "a reasonable amount of noise at predictable times," not a silent bird.

The Most Common Reasons Parrots Scream

  • It works. This is the big one. If every scream makes you walk over, talk to the bird, or even yell "be quiet," the parrot learns that screaming is an effective way to get you. Even negative attention is attention.
  • Loneliness or boredom. A social bird left alone in a quiet room with nothing to do will often fill the silence itself.
  • Too little sleep. Parrots generally need around 10 to 12 hours of dark, quiet rest. A bird kept up by the TV or room lights is often cranky and loud.
  • Fear or startle. Sudden noises, flashing lights, a new object near the cage or a pet that stalks the cage can trigger alarm calls.
  • Hormones. Seasonal hormonal changes can make some birds louder and more demanding for weeks.
  • Routine changes. Moving the cage, a new schedule or a new person in the house can all increase noise.
  • Not enough exercise or foraging. A bird with a lot of energy and no outlet will find one.

What to Do When the Screaming Starts

The core idea is simple: stop rewarding the scream and start rewarding quiet.

  • Do not yell back. To a flock animal, shouting looks like you are joining in. It usually makes things louder.
  • Wait for a pause, then reward it. Even two seconds of quiet counts at first. Walk over, offer a treat, or say a calm "good bird." Then slowly stretch the length of quiet you ask for.
  • Give attention before the scream, not after. Several short, calm check-ins during the day (talking, a small treat, a few minutes of interaction) teach the bird it does not need to shout to get you.
  • Do not rush in at every scream. If you must respond, make the response neutral and brief. A bird that screams and gets a long interaction every time will scream more.
  • Expect an extinction burst. When a behavior stops working, it often gets worse for a few days before it improves. Stay consistent; giving in just once teaches the bird that louder is better.

Fix the Environment Too

  • Protect sleep. Give a consistent bedtime in a quiet, dim room. A cage cover at night is fine for this purpose.
  • Add foraging. Hide treats in paper, cardboard or foraging toys so the bird spends time working for food instead of calling for you.
  • Rotate toys. Swap what is in the cage every week or two so things feel new.
  • Offer out-of-cage time every day. Flying, climbing and supervised time with you uses energy that otherwise becomes noise.
  • Keep a predictable routine. Wake-up, meals, play and bedtime at similar times help a bird feel secure.
  • Use covering carefully. A short quiet time-out under a cover is sometimes suggested, but it can backfire if the bird learns that screaming leads to the cover. Do not use it as a punishment.
  • Place the cage thoughtfully. Birds often do best where they can see the family but not in the middle of the busiest traffic.

When Screaming Is a Health Signal

Birds hide illness well. If the screaming is new, sounds different, comes with fluffed feathers, appetite changes, changes in droppings, breathing effort or lethargy, or the bird seems to be in pain, do not treat it as a training problem. See an avian veterinarian. Sudden feather plucking together with screaming also deserves a vet visit to rule out medical causes before you assume behavior is the issue. If the problem continues despite your best efforts, an avian vet or a qualified parrot behavior consultant can help.

A Realistic Timeline

  • Days 1 to 3: Note when the screaming happens and what comes right before and after it.
  • Week 1: Fix sleep, add foraging, and begin rewarding quiet moments.
  • Weeks 2 to 4: Expect some bad days. Stay consistent.
  • Month 2 onward: Most owners see a clear drop in demand screaming when everyone in the home follows the same rules.

Consistency matters more than any single trick. Everyone in the house needs to respond the same way, or the bird will simply learn which person gives in.
