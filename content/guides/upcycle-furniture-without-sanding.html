Why Sanding Isn't Actually the Requirement People Think It Is

The idea that you must sand furniture down to bare wood before painting or refinishing it is one of the most repeated pieces of DIY advice — and one of the most avoidable. Sanding exists to do one job: rough up a slick, glossy surface so new paint or stain has something to physically grip onto. If you handle that "grip" problem another way, you can skip the sanding step entirely, including on old dressers, tables, cabinets, and thrift-store finds with a factory finish, varnish, or lacquer.

That matters because sanding is usually the part of a furniture project that stops people before they start. It's dusty, slow, and hard to do evenly on carved details, turned legs, or veneer that can't take much abrasion. Once you know the no-sand alternatives, an old piece that's been sitting in a garage or basement becomes a weekend project instead of a multi-day chore.

Method 1: Deglosser (Liquid Sandpaper)

A liquid deglosser, sometimes sold as "liquid sandpaper," is a solvent that chemically etches a glossy finish so paint can bond to it. You wipe it on with a rag, let it work for the time listed on the label (usually a few minutes), and wipe off the residue. It won't strip color or remove damage, but it kills the shine and slickness that causes paint to peel later.

  • Work in a ventilated area — deglosser is a strong-smelling solvent, and gloves are worth wearing.
  • Apply it to the whole piece, including edges and undersides you might touch during use.
  • Wipe with the grain using a lint-free cloth, then let the piece dry fully before priming.

Method 2: A Bonding Primer

This is the single most important product in a no-sand furniture project. A bonding primer (sometimes labeled "adhesion primer") is formulated to stick to slick surfaces — laminate, varnish, lacquer, even melamine — without mechanical roughing. Once it's on and cured, your topcoat paint bonds to the primer instead of to the original finish, so the original surface's slickness stops being a problem.

Two thin coats generally outperform one thick one. A thick coat is more likely to drip into corners and stay tacky longer, which is exactly where peeling starts later.

Method 3: Chalk-Style or Mineral Paint

Chalk-style and mineral paints are popular specifically because many are formulated to adhere to existing finishes with minimal prep. They're thicker than regular latex paint and have a built-in grip that regular wall paint doesn't. On a piece in decent structural shape with an intact (not peeling or flaking) finish, some of these paints can go directly over a clean, degreased surface with no primer step at all — always check the specific product's label, since formulas vary.

The tradeoff is usually a matte, slightly textured look rather than the smooth factory finish you'd get from primer and enamel. For farmhouse, vintage, or distressed-style projects, that's often exactly the look people want anyway.

Method 4: Gel Stain Over an Existing Stain

If the goal is a new wood tone rather than paint, gel stain is the no-sand option. Unlike traditional penetrating stain, gel stain sits more on the surface than soaking into the wood grain, which means it can go over an existing stained or varnished finish and still change the color convincingly. It's especially useful for pieces where the existing color is uneven, sun-faded in spots, or has minor water rings.

Apply it with a rag or brush, work it into the grain, then wipe off the excess before it sets — leaving too much on the surface is the most common mistake and leads to a tacky, uneven finish.

Getting the Surface Ready — Cleaning Matters More Than People Expect

Skipping sanding raises the importance of cleaning. Furniture that's been in a kitchen, garage, or smoker's home almost always has a thin film of grease, cooking residue, nicotine, or wax on it, even when it looks clean. Paint and primer bond poorly to that film regardless of how good the product is.

  • Wash with a degreasing cleaner (a diluted dish soap solution or a dedicated TSP substitute works well) rather than just water.
  • Rinse and let the piece dry completely — trapped moisture under paint causes bubbling later.
  • Wipe with a tack cloth right before priming to catch any remaining dust or lint.

When You Should Still Sand — At Least a Little

No-sand methods work best on finishes that are intact. There are situations where some light sanding genuinely is the right call:

  • Peeling or flaking finish: Loose paint or varnish needs to be scraped or lightly sanded off first — new paint bonded to a flaking layer will peel with it.
  • Very glossy lacquer: A quick pass with fine-grit sandpaper (220-grit) alongside deglosser gives extra insurance on especially slick finishes.
  • Deep gouges or water damage: These need to be filled and leveled regardless of your paint method, which usually means some localized sanding.

In these cases, you don't need to strip the whole piece to bare wood — a light scuff-sand of the problem areas, combined with a bonding primer everywhere else, is usually enough.

A Simple No-Sand Workflow

  • Clean and degrease the entire piece; let it dry fully.
  • Apply deglosser if the finish is glossy or slick, and wipe clean.
  • Apply two thin coats of bonding primer, letting each dry per the label.
  • Paint with your chosen topcoat, or use gel stain if you want a wood-tone finish instead of paint.
  • Seal with a clear topcoat or wax if the piece will see daily use, like a tabletop or dresser top.

Turning One Piece Into a Habit
