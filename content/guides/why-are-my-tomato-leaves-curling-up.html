The short answer

In the vast majority of home gardens, upward-curling tomato leaves are the plant protecting itself from heat and water stress, not a disease. When roots can't keep up with how fast a leaf is losing moisture through its surface, the leaf rolls lengthwise to shrink its exposed area and slow that water loss down. It usually starts on the lower, older leaves first, and the leaves keep their normal green color and shape once you gently uncurl one with your fingers.

That said, curling can also come from a few other causes that need a different fix, so it's worth spending five minutes checking which one you're actually dealing with before you change anything about how you're watering or feeding the plant.

Cause 1: Heat and light stress (the most common cause)

Tomatoes start curling their leaves upward once daytime temperatures climb into the high 80s and 90s Fahrenheit, especially during a stretch of several hot, sunny days in a row. If you're growing under a grow light, a fixture positioned too close can create the same localized heat stress even indoors. The leaf curl is a defense mechanism, similar to how a plant might otherwise wilt, and it typically eases up once temperatures drop or the plant is given some afternoon shade.

  • Check the weather: has it been unusually hot for the past 3-5 days?
  • If growing under lights, check the distance from the canopy to the bulb and raise it if it's closer than the manufacturer recommends.
  • Look at which leaves are curling — lower leaves curling first, while new growth at the top still looks normal, points strongly toward heat/water stress rather than a virus.

Cause 2: Watering that's inconsistent in either direction

Both too little and too much water can cause the same upward curl, which is why "just water more" sometimes makes things worse instead of better. Underwatering is the more common culprit outdoors in summer — the plant curls its leaves to cut water loss while the roots try to catch up. Overwatering causes a different kind of stress: soggy, oxygen-starved roots can't actually take up water efficiently even though there's plenty in the soil, so the plant shows the same drought-like curling symptom.

  • Stick a finger 2-3 inches into the soil near the root zone. Bone dry means underwatering; consistently soggy or muddy means overwatering.
  • Deep, less-frequent watering (soaking the root zone thoroughly 2-3 times a week) beats light daily sprinkling for mature tomato plants.
  • Make sure containers and raised beds actually drain — a pot or box with no drainage holes will waterlog roots even if you're watering a "normal" amount.
  • A 2-3 inch layer of mulch around the base slows soil moisture swings between waterings and reduces stress-curling significantly.

Cause 3: Heavy pruning or too much nitrogen fertilizer

If you recently did a hard prune — removing a lot of suckers or lower branches at once — the remaining leaves can curl for a week or two while the plant rebalances how much leaf area it's supporting. Similarly, tomatoes that get a heavy dose of high-nitrogen fertilizer often respond with fast, leggy, curled new growth. Nitrogen pushes rapid leaf and stem growth faster than the plant's vascular system can properly supply, and curling is one of the visible signs of that mismatch.

  • If you fertilized in the last week or two with a high-nitrogen product, hold off on the next feeding and switch to a balanced or lower-nitrogen formula going forward.
  • After a hard prune, give the plant 1-2 weeks to recover before assuming something else is wrong.

Cause 4: Sap-sucking pests

Aphids, whiteflies, and thrips feed by piercing the leaf and drawing out sap, and that feeding damage can cause localized curling — usually paired with other visible clues, unlike simple heat stress.

  • Flip curled leaves over and check the undersides and stem joints for small clustered insects, sticky residue (honeydew), or fine webbing.
  • A strong blast of water from a hose, followed by insecticidal soap for a repeat infestation, handles most light-to-moderate cases.
  • Ants traveling up and down the stem are often a tell — they farm aphids for the honeydew, so ant traffic is a hint to check closely for aphids even if you don't spot them right away.

Cause 5: Herbicide drift or viral infection (less common, but worth ruling out)

Two less common causes are worth knowing about because they need a different response than the ones above.

  • Herbicide exposure — even drift from a neighbor's lawn treatment, or residue in compost/grass clippings made from treated lawns — can cause dramatic, twisted curling along with distorted new growth. If curling is severe and appeared suddenly with no heat or watering change, this is worth considering, especially if nearby plants show similar symptoms.
  • Viral infections like Tobacco Mosaic Virus cause curling paired with a mottled, mosaic-like discoloration on the leaves and stunted, distorted new growth — not just a clean roll of an otherwise normal-looking leaf. There's no cure for a viral infection; affected plants should be removed to protect the rest of the garden, and tools used on them should be disinfected before touching other plants.

How to tell which cause you're dealing with

Work through these questions in order — they cover the large majority of real-world cases:

  • Is it hot, and are mostly the lower/older leaves curling with normal color? → Almost certainly heat/water stress. Adjust watering and consider afternoon shade; it should ease up within days.
  • Did you recently prune hard or fertilize heavily? → Give it 1-2 weeks before troubleshooting further.
  • Do you see insects, sticky residue, or ants on the stems? → Treat for pests.
  • Is the curling severe, twisted, or paired with mottled/discolored leaves and stunted new growth? → Rule out herbicide drift or a viral infection; consider removing badly affected plants.

The reassuring part

For the majority of home gardeners, tomato leaf curl from heat or watering stress doesn't reduce the final harvest much, if at all — the plant is simply managing water loss, and it typically returns to normal leaf shape once conditions even out. It's a symptom worth watching rather than an emergency worth panicking over, as long as you rule out the pest, herbicide, and virus possibilities above.

If you're finding that keeping a backyard vegetable patch healthy and productive feels like a constant round of troubleshooting — curling leaves one week, something else the next — it usually comes down to how the whole small-space garden is set up from the start: soil, spacing, watering routine, and what you're growing where. A structured, step-by-step approach to building out a productive backyard food garden can take a lot of the guesswork out of it so problems like this become rare instead of routine.
