Your new puppy came home, and the first few days were fine. Then bedtime comes, the crate door closes, and the crying starts. It might be a soft whine, or it might be howling that goes on until 3 a.m. Almost every new puppy owner goes through this. It is one of the most common early problems, and one of the most fixable. The goal isn't to make your puppy "stop complaining." You want to teach them that the crate is a safe place to sleep, and you want to meet the needs that are actually causing some of that noise.

Why puppies cry in the crate at night

For most of their short lives, puppies have slept in a warm pile of littermates, with their mother nearby. Your house is the first place they have ever been alone at night. The crying usually comes from one or more of these causes:

  • Isolation. Puppies are wired to call out when separated from the group. This is normal, and it fades with time and gentle practice.
  • A full bladder. Young puppies physically can't hold it all night. A common rule of thumb is that a puppy can hold it for about their age in months plus one hour, up to a limit. That means an 8–10 week old puppy usually needs a trip outside in the middle of the night.
  • Too much energy at bedtime. A puppy who napped all evening and then gets shut in the crate isn't tired enough to settle.
  • Discomfort. The crate may be too hot, too cold, drafty, or near a noisy appliance. The puppy may also be hungry, or teething.
  • A crate that means "abandonment." If the only time the crate is used is when everyone leaves or goes to bed, the puppy learns that the crate predicts being alone.
  • Learned crying. If crying has been followed by being let out to play, the puppy has learned that crying works.

Set up the crate so it's easy to settle

A few changes to the setup often cut the crying in half before any training starts.

  • Put the crate in your bedroom at first. Hearing and smelling you nearby is the single biggest comfort for a young puppy. You can move the crate gradually to its long-term spot once your puppy sleeps through the night.
  • Get the size right. The puppy should be able to stand, turn around, and lie down stretched out, but not have so much room that one end becomes a bathroom. Many wire crates come with a divider panel you can move as the puppy grows.
  • Make it cozy. Add soft bedding the puppy can't easily shred. Some owners put a cover over three sides so the crate feels more like a den. A piece of cloth that smells like the litter or like you can also help.
  • Consider gentle background sound. A fan or white-noise machine can mask household noises that startle a puppy awake.
  • Keep it safe. Don't leave anything inside that could be chewed apart and swallowed. Remove collars with tags, which can catch on wire.

Build a bedtime routine

Puppies settle more easily when bedtime is predictable. A simple routine looks like this:

  • Play and a bit of training 30–60 minutes before bed to use up energy. Short sniffing games and a few minutes of "sit" and "down" work tire a puppy's brain as well as its body.
  • Stop food about 2–3 hours before bedtime and limit big drinks in the last hour, unless your vet has advised otherwise. Don't restrict water too much, especially in hot weather.
  • A calm wind-down period. Lower the energy in the house for 15–20 minutes before bed.
  • A final potty trip right before the crate. Go out on leash to the same spot, wait quietly, and praise calmly when they go.
  • Into the crate with something to do. A safe chew or a food toy with a small portion of their kibble gives the puppy a pleasant, calming activity while they drift off.

What to do when the crying starts

This is the part everyone asks about: should you let a puppy cry it out? The honest answer is "not for long, and not every time." Leaving a young puppy to scream for an hour usually backfires. The puppy may get so worked up that they soil the crate, and they can come to fear it. On the other hand, rushing over at every squeak teaches them that noise brings you back. The middle path works best:

  • Give it a few minutes. Many puppies whine briefly and then fall asleep. If the noise is dropping off, stay quiet and let them settle.
  • Respond to escalating or persistent crying. If crying keeps building, or goes on past about 10–15 minutes, assume there's a real need, especially if it has been a few hours since the last potty break.
  • Make potty trips boring. Take the puppy out on leash to the same spot, with no play, no chatting, and no treats beyond quiet praise for going. Then return them straight to the crate. The message is that nighttime means sleep, not party time.
  • Offer calm reassurance without letting them out. With the crate next to your bed, you can often calm a puppy just by dangling your fingers near the door or saying a few soft words. Over several nights, do less and less of this.
  • Try to wait for a pause. When possible, open the crate during a brief quiet moment rather than at the peak of the howling. This slowly teaches that calm, not noise, gets results.

Set an alarm for a middle-of-the-night potty break during the first weeks. Taking the puppy out before they wake up needing to go prevents a lot of crying. As your puppy grows, push that alarm later by 15–30 minutes every few nights until they sleep through.

Make the crate a good place during the day

Most nighttime crate problems are really daytime crate problems. If the crate only appears at bedtime, it becomes a signal that fun is over and everyone is leaving. Change that association:

  • Feed meals inside the crate with the door open.
  • Toss treats into the crate randomly throughout the day so your puppy finds surprises there.
  • Practice short, calm closed-door sessions while you sit nearby: a few seconds at first, then minutes, while your puppy works on a chew.
  • Use the crate for daytime naps, too, not only at night or when you leave the house.
  • Never use the crate as punishment.

How long does this phase last?

With a consistent setup and routine, many puppies cry noticeably less within the first 3–7 nights, and most settle within two to three weeks. Some puppies, especially those who came home very young or had a stressful start, take longer. Progress is rarely a straight line. A rough night after a good one is normal, often caused by a missed nap, a change in routine, or teething.

When to talk to a vet or trainer

Most crate crying is normal adjustment. But please check with your veterinarian if your puppy suddenly needs to urinate much more often than usual, strains, has diarrhea, vomits, or seems to be in pain. These can point to a urinary tract infection, parasites, or another medical issue, not a training problem. If your puppy panics in the crate, drools heavily, tries to chew or dig their way out, or hurts themselves, stop crate training and talk to your vet or a qualified, positive-reinforcement trainer. Signs like these can point to real separation-related distress, which needs a more careful plan.

Putting it together

A puppy crying in the crate at night is usually telling you something simple: "I'm alone," "I need to pee," or "I'm not tired yet." Put the crate near you, build a predictable bedtime routine, keep night potty trips short and dull, and spend daytime making the crate a place your puppy wants to be. Nighttime crying is closely tied to potty training, since a puppy who has learned to hold it and to go on schedule sleeps far better.
