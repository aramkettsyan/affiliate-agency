The Short Answer: Plan for 3-4 Square Feet Per Hen Inside

If you're sketching a backyard coop and wondering how big it really has to be, the commonly cited rule for standard-size hens is about 3 to 4 square feet of indoor floor space per bird, plus roughly 8 to 10 square feet per bird in an outdoor run. Most keepers who regret their coop regret making it too small, so when in doubt, size up. Chickens that are crowded get stressed, peck each other, and make a muddy, smelly mess faster than you'd expect.

Space Needs by Chicken Type

The 3-4 square foot figure is a starting point, not a law. Adjust for the birds you actually keep:

  • Standard hens (Leghorns, Rhode Island Reds, Orpingtons on the smaller side): about 3-4 square feet each inside.
  • Large, heavy breeds (Plymouth Rock, Brahma, Jersey Giant): at least 4 square feet each, and more is better.
  • Bantams: roughly 2 square feet each inside if they have regular outdoor access.
  • Birds that are shut in most of the day: lean toward the top of the range, or beyond it.

Cold climates deserve special mention. If your winters regularly drop below freezing, your hens will spend far more time indoors, so adding a few extra square feet per bird makes a real difference to behavior and cleanliness.

What About the Run?

Coop space is where birds sleep, lay, and shelter. The run is where they scratch, dust-bathe, and forage. Plan about 8 to 10 square feet of run per bird as a minimum. If the run is small, bare dirt turns to mud quickly, and mud is a common cause of dirty eggs and foot problems. If your run is on the smaller side, consider rotating sections, adding deep mulch or wood chips, or giving the flock supervised free-range time when you're home.

Example Sizes for Common Flocks

Here is what the math looks like for standard-size hens at 4 square feet each, which gives a comfortable cushion:

  • 3 hens: about 12 square feet indoors (3 ft x 4 ft), with a 24-30 square foot run.
  • 6 hens: about 24 square feet indoors (4 ft x 6 ft), with a 48-60 square foot run.
  • 10 hens: about 40 square feet indoors (5 ft x 8 ft), with an 80-100 square foot run.

A neat trick: build the coop for one or two more birds than you plan to start with. Almost everybody ends up adding chickens, and expanding a finished coop is harder than building it a bit larger on day one.

Space Is More Than Floor Area

Floor area is only part of the picture. A coop that hits the numbers on paper can still feel crowded if the interior is laid out badly. Check these as well:

  • Roost space: allow roughly 8 to 12 inches of roost bar per bird, set higher than the nesting boxes so hens sleep on the bar, not in the boxes.
  • Nesting boxes: one box for every 3 to 4 hens is plenty, since hens tend to favor a couple of favorites anyway.
  • Ventilation: openings high up, above roost height, let moisture and ammonia escape without blowing a draft directly on sleeping birds.
  • Access for cleaning: you should be able to reach every corner with a scraper or shovel. If you can't, the coop will not get cleaned as often as it should.

Signs Your Coop Is Too Small

Birds will tell you if they don't have enough room. Watch for feather pecking or bald patches, hens bullying the same flock-mate, a constant fight at the feeder or waterer, damp bedding that smells of ammonia even with regular cleaning, and hens refusing to go in at night because there's no good spot on the roost. Any of these is a cue to add space, thin the flock, or increase outdoor time before it becomes a bigger problem.

Check Local Rules Before You Build

Many towns limit the number of hens you can keep, require a minimum distance from property lines, or ask for a permit for coops over a certain size. Check your municipal code or HOA rules before buying lumber. It's a five-minute search that can save you from tearing down a finished coop.

A Note on Bird Health

This guide covers housing, not medical care. If your hens show sudden lethargy, labored breathing, swollen eyes, or unusual death in the flock, contact a veterinarian who sees poultry or your local agricultural extension office. Overcrowding can contribute to disease spread, but it is rarely the only factor.
