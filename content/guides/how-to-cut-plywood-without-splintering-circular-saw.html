You measure twice, clamp the sheet, make the cut, and flip the panel over to find a ragged edge of torn veneer running the full length of the line. Plywood splintering (woodworkers call it tearout) is one of the most common frustrations with a circular saw, and it is especially painful on cabinet-grade sheets where the face veneer is thinner than a business card. The good news is that tearout is predictable. Once you understand why it happens, a few cheap habits will give you edges clean enough to show.

Why plywood splinters in the first place

Plywood is built from thin layers of wood veneer glued with the grain of each layer running crosswise to the next. The top face veneer is very thin and its fibers are only held down by glue. When a saw tooth exits the material, it pushes those surface fibers outward instead of slicing them, and with nothing behind them for support they break away in chips and splinters.

The key detail for a circular saw is direction. A circular saw blade spins upward through the material, entering from the bottom and exiting through the top face. That means the top face, the one you are looking at while you cut, is the side that tears out. A table saw is the opposite: the blade comes down through the top, so the bottom face takes the damage. Almost every fix below works by either supporting the fibers where the teeth exit or making sure they are cut cleanly before the tooth can pull them.

1. Put the good face down

This is the single easiest change and costs nothing. With a circular saw, lay the sheet with its show face down, so any tearout lands on the back side that will be hidden inside a cabinet, against a wall, or underneath a shelf. Mark your cut line on the back face. If both faces will be visible, combine this with the other steps below rather than relying on it alone.

2. Use the right blade, and keep it sharp

The general-purpose blade that came with your saw is usually a 24-tooth framing blade. It is designed to cut quickly through lumber, and each tooth takes a big bite, which is exactly what tears plywood veneer. For plywood, look for a blade sold for plywood or fine finish work with a much higher tooth count (often 40 to 60 teeth or more on a 7-1/4 inch blade). More teeth means each tooth removes less material and leaves a cleaner edge.

  • A dull blade tears instead of cuts. If an older blade suddenly starts leaving fuzzy edges or burning, it likely needs sharpening or replacing.
  • Clean off pitch and glue buildup; gummed-up teeth behave like dull ones.
  • Set the blade depth so it extends only about 1/4 inch below the sheet. A shallower blade meets the surface at a steeper angle, which tends to slice the fibers instead of lifting them.

3. Score the cut line first

Before you cut, run a sharp utility knife along the line, pressed against a straightedge, two or three times. This severs the top veneer fibers so the blade has nothing to lift. Make the saw cut on the waste side so the kerf runs right along your scored line. On expensive hardwood plywood this step alone often eliminates most visible tearout.

Some woodworkers also make a very shallow first pass with the saw (set to just a few millimeters deep), then lower the blade and make the full cut. This works best with a guide rail so both passes follow exactly the same line.

4. Tape the line

Painter's or masking tape over the cut line helps hold surface fibers in place as the teeth exit. Press it down firmly, mark your line on the tape, and cut through it. Peel it off slowly afterward, pulling back toward the cut edge at a low angle, so you do not lift loose veneer along with it. Low-tack painter's tape is the safer choice on delicate veneers. Tape is not a cure-all, but it is a quick improvement on its own and a good addition to scoring.

5. Support the sheet properly

A sagging sheet pinches the blade, vibrates, and makes the saw wander, all of which increase tearout and are also a real kickback hazard. The cleanest and safest method for cutting full sheets is to lay the plywood on a sheet of rigid foam insulation board on the floor or on a pair of sawhorses topped with 2x4s, then set the blade to cut just into the foam. The sheet is supported on both sides of the cut, the offcut cannot drop and splinter the last few inches, and the foam backs up the fibers along the line.

  • Never support a sheet only at its ends with the cut in the middle; it will close on the blade.
  • Support the offcut so it does not break away at the end of the cut and tear a corner off.

6. Guide the saw with a straightedge

Freehand cuts wobble slightly, and every wobble rubs the blade sideways against the edge, which chips veneer. Clamp a straight board or a factory-edge strip of plywood to the sheet as a fence and run the saw base against it. A simple shop-made cutting guide (a narrow fence glued to a wider base that you trim with your own saw) is one of the most useful jigs you can build: its edge shows you exactly where the blade will cut, and it presses the fibers down right next to the kerf.

7. Add zero-clearance support at the saw base

The opening in a circular saw's base plate is wide, so the fibers right beside the blade are unsupported. Attaching a thin piece of hardboard (about 1/4 inch) to the base with double-sided tape and then plunging the running blade through it creates a slot that exactly matches your blade. That piece then presses down on the veneer right at the point where the teeth exit. Follow your saw manufacturer's instructions and make sure the guard still moves freely before using any modified base.

8. Control your feed speed

Pushing too fast makes each tooth take a bigger bite; pushing too slow can burn the edge. Let the blade reach full speed before it touches the wood, then feed at a steady, moderate pace without forcing it. If the motor bogs down, you are going too fast. Keep feeding all the way through the end of the cut rather than slowing or stopping, which is where many of the worst splinters happen.

When a track saw or table saw makes more sense

If you break down plywood often, a track saw is designed for exactly this job: its rail has a splinter strip that supports the fibers right along the cut and keeps the saw perfectly straight. A table saw with a zero-clearance insert and a good plywood blade also gives very clean cuts on smaller panels. But for most hobbyists, a circular saw with a good blade, a guide, and the habits above produces results that are hard to tell apart.

If you already have tearout

For small chips on an edge that will be covered, edge banding or a thin solid-wood strip will hide them completely. For minor chips on a painted project, wood filler sanded flush works well. If the damage is on a show face that will be stained, the cleanest fix is usually to recut the piece slightly oversize with the methods above and then trim to final size, since filler rarely takes stain the same way as veneer.

A quick safety note

Several of these tips involve clamping, changing blades, and modifying how a saw is set up. Always unplug the saw or remove the battery before changing blades or attaching anything to the base, wear eye and hearing protection, keep both hands on the saw during the cut, and never disable or wedge the blade guard. If you are new to circular saws, review your saw's manual before trying anything unfamiliar.
