You cut four pieces at 45 degrees, dry-fit the picture frame, and there it is: a sliver of light at one corner, or worse, the inside of the joint closes while the outside gapes open. Miters look like the simplest joint in woodworking, two angled cuts meeting at a corner, but they are unforgiving. Small errors don't stay small; they add up around the frame and show at the last corner you close. The good news is that miter gaps almost always come from a handful of causes you can diagnose in a few minutes.

First, figure out which kind of gap you have

Before you change anything, look closely at the shape of the gap. Each shape points to a different cause.

  • Open on the outside, tight on the inside: your combined angle is more than 90 degrees. Each cut is slightly under 45 degrees (for example 44.5), so the heel of the joint meets but the toe doesn't.
  • Open on the inside, tight on the outside: the opposite. Your cuts are slightly over 45 degrees and the corner closes up at the outer tip first.
  • Open at the top or bottom face only: the blade isn't square to the table (a bevel problem), or the stock was rocking or not flat when you cut.
  • Three corners tight, one corner open: usually the opposite sides of the frame aren't exactly the same length, so the frame becomes a slight parallelogram.
  • Tight when built, open months later: wood movement. The joint was fine; the wood shrank across its width.

Cause 1: The saw isn't truly at 45 degrees

The detent on a miter saw or the scale on a table saw miter gauge is a starting point, not a guarantee. Half a degree of error doesn't sound like much, but a four-sided frame has eight cut faces. Half a degree on each one adds up to a four-degree error across the frame, and it all shows up as a gap at the final corner.

The fix is to test, not trust. Cut two pieces of scrap at your 45 setting, put them together, and check the resulting corner with a reliable square. If the corner is open on the outside, increase the angle slightly; if it's open on the inside, decrease it. A cheap trick: cut four short pieces, assemble a small test frame, and look at the last corner, since that one shows you the total accumulated error.

Cause 2: The blade isn't square to the table

If the joint is tight on one face and open on the other, check the bevel. Set a small engineer's square or speed square against the blade body (between teeth) and the saw table. Even a degree off vertical will make the joint close on one face and open on the other. Adjust the bevel stop per your saw's manual and re-test on scrap.

Cause 3: The workpiece moved during the cut

Miter saw blades tend to pull the workpiece toward or away from the blade as they cut, especially on narrow trim. A few habits help a lot:

  • Hold the stock firmly against the fence and table, or clamp it. Keep your hand at a safe distance from the blade path.
  • Let the blade reach full speed before cutting, and come down slowly and steadily.
  • Let the blade stop fully before lifting it out of the cut, which avoids nicking the fresh face.
  • Use a sharp, fine-tooth crosscut blade. A dull or coarse blade deflects and leaves a rough face that won't close tight.

Also check your stock. If a piece is bowed or twisted, it can rock during the cut, and the angle you cut won't be the angle you think. Flat, straight stock makes everything easier.

Cause 4: Opposite sides aren't identical in length

On a frame or box, the angles can be perfect and you'll still get a gap if the two long sides or the two short sides differ by even a fraction of a millimeter. Measuring each piece separately with a tape is where this usually creeps in.

The fix is to stop measuring opposite pieces individually. Instead, use a stop block clamped to the saw fence or a sled, and cut both matching pieces against the same stop without moving it. Matching parts end up identical, which is what actually matters for a closed frame.

Cause 5: The wood moved after glue-up

Wood expands and shrinks across its width with changes in humidity, but barely changes along its length. In a miter, the two pieces meet with their grain running in different directions, so when they shrink they pull the joint open, usually at the inside corner. This is extremely common on door and window casing and on frames built in a humid shop and then brought into a dry, heated house.

You can reduce it by letting lumber acclimate in the room where the piece will live for several days before you cut, and by finishing all faces evenly so moisture moves in and out more slowly. For trim that keeps opening seasonally, some builders switch to corner blocks or rosettes, which avoid the miter altogether.

How to fix gaps you already have

What you do depends on how big the gap is and whether the piece is already glued.

  • Not glued yet: re-cut. Take a whisker off the long side of the offending piece with the saw angle adjusted, or use a shooting board with a hand plane to trim the face a few passes at a time until it closes. Glue and clamps will not pull a badly fitting miter together permanently.
  • Hairline gaps on a glued joint: rub the corner firmly with the round shank of a screwdriver to burnish the wood fibers over the gap, then sand lightly. This works well on softwoods and painted trim.
  • Small gaps on a stained or clear-finished piece: fill with fine sanding dust from the same board mixed with a little glue, or with a color-matched wood filler, then sand flush. Test the color on scrap first, because filler often finishes differently from the surrounding wood.
  • Painted work: paintable caulk or wood putty, sanded smooth, is quick and nearly invisible once painted.
  • Larger gaps or weak joints: cut a kerf across the corner and glue in a spline (a thin slip of wood). It adds strength and can be a decorative contrast. The most thorough fix is to cut the frame apart and remake the joint.

Glue-up tips so tight miters stay tight

  • Dry-fit the whole assembly with the clamps you plan to use before any glue goes on.
  • Miter end grain soaks up glue. Brush a thin coat on both faces, wait a minute, then apply a second coat before assembling to avoid a starved joint.
  • Use a band clamp or corner clamps that pull all four corners in evenly. Clamping one corner at a time can push the frame out of square.
  • Check diagonals: on a square or rectangular frame, the two diagonal measurements should match. If they don't, nudge the long diagonal in before the glue sets.
  • For joints that need real strength, add splines, biscuits, or dowels. A plain glued miter is end grain to end grain and relatively weak on its own.

Power tools are involved in almost all of this. Follow your saw's safety instructions, keep guards in place, and wear eye and hearing protection.

Practice on projects designed for it

Most people get good at miters by building a few small frames and boxes where the dimensions are already worked out, so they can focus on technique instead of math.
