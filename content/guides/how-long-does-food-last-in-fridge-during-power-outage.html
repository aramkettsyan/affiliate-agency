The lights go out, the fridge hum stops, and the first question is always the same: how long do I have before the food in there is a problem? The short answer from US food-safety guidance is about four hours for the refrigerator and 24 to 48 hours for the freezer, but only if you handle things correctly. Here is how those numbers work in real life, and what to do in the first few hours.

The basic timeline

These are the figures commonly given by the USDA and FoodSafety.gov, and they all assume the doors stay shut:

  • Refrigerator: perishable food stays safe for about 4 hours.
  • Full freezer: roughly 48 hours.
  • Half-full freezer: roughly 24 hours.

The clock starts when the power goes off, not when you notice. If you came home to a dark house, you may not know how long it has been out, and that matters a lot for what you do next.

Why the door matters so much

A closed fridge is basically an insulated box holding cold air and cold food. Every time you open the door, cold air spills out and warm room air flows in. Opening it "just to check" a few times can cut your safe window noticeably. Decide what you need once, open quickly, and close it.

A full freezer lasts longer than a half-empty one because frozen items keep each other cold. If you have empty space in your freezer, filling it with containers of water that you freeze ahead of time is a cheap way to extend the window in the future.

What to do in the first hour

  • Leave the doors closed and note the time the power went out.
  • Check the outage estimate from your utility so you can plan around it.
  • Group items in the freezer together so they stay cold as a block.
  • If you have a cooler, start filling it with ice for the most important refrigerated items such as milk, meat and leftovers.
  • Keep an appliance thermometer in both the fridge and freezer. The fridge should be at or below 40°F (4°C) and the freezer at 0°F (-18°C). Read it without holding the door open.

What to throw out after four hours

Once a refrigerator has been without power for more than four hours, the guidance is to discard perishable food that has been above 40°F. That mainly means:

  • Raw and cooked meat, poultry and fish
  • Eggs and egg-based dishes
  • Milk, soft cheeses, yogurt and cream
  • Cooked leftovers, cut fruit and prepared salads

Some foods are more forgiving: hard cheeses, butter, whole uncut fruit and vegetables, and most condiments such as mustard, ketchup and jam generally hold up fine. When in doubt, throw it out. You cannot judge safety by smell or taste, because the bacteria that make people sick often do not change how food smells.

Can you refreeze thawed food?

If food in the freezer still has ice crystals or is at 40°F or below, it can usually be refrozen, though quality such as texture may suffer. If it has fully thawed and warmed up, especially meat or seafood, it is safer to discard it. Ice cream that has melted and refrozen should go in the bin.

Using dry ice and ice for longer outages

For an outage that stretches past a day, ice becomes your friend. A block of ice in a cooler can keep refrigerated items cold for many hours, and dry ice can extend a freezer's safe window by a day or more. Dry ice needs care: wear gloves, never touch it with bare hands, and use it in a ventilated space because it releases carbon dioxide gas. Never store it in a sealed container.

Food safety is a health issue

Eating spoiled food can cause serious illness, especially for infants, older adults, pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system. If someone develops vomiting, diarrhea, fever or other worrying symptoms after eating food from a warm fridge, contact a doctor or your local health service. This article is general information, not medical advice.

Planning ahead so the next outage is less stressful

Most people only think about this after the first long outage. A few small habits make the next one much easier:

  • Keep an appliance thermometer in your fridge and freezer.
  • Freeze water in reusable containers to fill empty freezer space.
  • Keep a cooler and a bag of ice or reusable ice packs ready.
  • Know where you can buy ice or dry ice nearby.
  • Keep a stock of shelf-stable food and water that does not depend on refrigeration.
  • Have a way to keep phones and a radio charged so you can follow updates.

The last two points are where outages go from an inconvenience to a real problem. A multi-day blackout, whether from a storm, a grid failure or something rarer, is about more than the fridge. It affects lighting, heating, communication and keeping electronics working.
