When the power goes out in winter, most people's first instinct is to try to keep the whole house warm. That's usually a losing battle. A typical house has far too much air, glass and outside wall to hold heat for long without a running furnace. The approach that actually works, and the one emergency-management and university extension guides keep coming back to, is simpler: pick one room, seal it up, and put all your heat and all your people in there.

This guide walks through how to choose that room, how to stop it losing heat, which heat sources are safe to use indoors, and the carbon monoxide mistakes that kill people every winter.

How long will a house stay warm without power?

There's no single number, because it depends on how well insulated the house is, how cold it is outside, and how windy it is. A well-insulated, newer home might only drop a few degrees in the first several hours. An older, drafty house in freezing wind can feel cold within a few hours and get uncomfortably cold overnight.

The useful takeaway is this: the heat stored in your house is a resource that starts draining the moment the furnace stops. The sooner you start conserving it, the longer it lasts. Don't wait until the house feels cold to act. Start in the first hour.

Step 1: Choose the right room

The best "warm room" is usually:

  • Small. Less air to heat, and your own body heat makes a bigger difference.
  • Interior, or on the side of the house away from the wind, with as few exterior walls as possible.
  • Low on windows. Glass loses heat much faster than an insulated wall.
  • On a lower floor if you have no heat source. Heat rises, but upper floors under the roof often lose heat fastest.
  • Close to a bathroom, so people aren't constantly opening doors to the rest of the cold house.

If you have a safe, working fireplace or a wood stove that's rated for indoor use, that room often becomes the obvious choice, even if it's bigger than ideal. In that case, close it off from the rest of the house as much as you can.

Step 2: Close off the rest of the house

  • Shut the doors to every room you're not using.
  • Roll up towels or blankets and push them against the bottom of those doors to stop cold air creeping in.
  • If your warm room has an open doorway with no door, hang a heavy blanket, a moving blanket or a shower curtain across it.
  • Keep exterior doors shut. Every trip outside lets a big slug of warm air escape.

Step 3: Stop the heat leaking out

Once you've picked the room, treat it like a cooler you're trying to keep warm.

  • Windows: close curtains and blinds, especially at night. If you have extra blankets, comforters or bubble wrap, hang or tape them over the glass for an extra layer.
  • Sunny windows: during the day, open the curtains on any window getting direct sun, then close them again as soon as the sun moves off.
  • Drafts: run your hand around windows, outlets on exterior walls and the edges of doors. Stuff gaps with towels or rags.
  • Floors: hard floors pull heat out of your body. Put down rugs, sleeping pads, folded blankets or even flattened cardboard where people sit and sleep.

Step 4: Make a "room within a room"

If it's very cold, go one step smaller. A tent pitched indoors, a blanket fort over a table, or a bed with blankets draped over a frame traps a pocket of air that your bodies can warm up quickly. Campers know this trick well: a small space with several people in it can be noticeably warmer than the room around it. Kids often find this part fun, which helps.

Step 5: Keep people warm, not just the room

  • Dress in loose layers. Tight clothing restricts circulation. Wool and fleece hold warmth even when slightly damp; cotton doesn't.
  • Wear a hat and dry socks, and change socks if they get damp.
  • Sleep together under shared blankets. A sleeping bag rated for cold weather is one of the best low-tech items you can own.
  • Eat and drink. Your body makes heat from food. Warm drinks help you feel better, though they don't replace real insulation.
  • Move a little. Light activity warms you up, but avoid sweating, because damp clothes make you colder afterward.
  • Fill water bottles with hot water (if you can heat water safely) and put them in sleeping bags, wrapped in a sock or towel so they don't burn skin.

Which heat sources are safe indoors?

This is where people get hurt. Carbon monoxide (CO) is colorless and odorless, and it's one of the leading causes of death during winter power outages. Follow these rules:

  • Never use a gas stove or oven to heat your home.
  • Never run a generator, grill, camp stove or charcoal inside the house, garage, basement or any partly enclosed area, even with doors or windows open. Keep generators well away from the house (many safety agencies say at least 20 feet) and away from windows and vents.
  • Only use fuel-burning space heaters that are specifically rated for indoor use, and follow the manufacturer's ventilation instructions exactly.
  • Use a fireplace or wood stove only if it's been inspected and the chimney is clear. Keep a screen in front of an open fire.
  • Candles add very little real heat and are a fire risk. If you use them for light, never leave them unattended and keep them away from blankets and curtains. Battery lanterns are safer.
  • Have battery-powered CO alarms. Hardwired alarms may not work without power unless they have a battery backup. Check them before winter.

If anyone gets a headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion or feels unusually sleepy while a fuel-burning heat source is running, get everyone into fresh air immediately and call emergency services.

Don't forget the pipes

A long outage in freezing weather can freeze and burst water pipes, which turns a cold night into an expensive repair. If the outage looks like it will last, open the cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls, and let faucets drip slightly. If the house is going to get close to freezing and you're leaving, consider shutting off the main water valve and draining the lines. Know where your main shutoff is before you need it.

When to leave

Staying home isn't always the right call. Consider going to a warming center, shelter, or a friend's house with power if:

  • Your warm room is dropping toward freezing and you have no safe heat source.
  • You have infants, elderly family members, or anyone with a medical condition that cold makes worse, or anyone who relies on powered medical equipment.
  • Anyone shows signs of hypothermia: intense shivering that stops, slurred speech, confusion, clumsiness or drowsiness.

Hypothermia and frostbite are medical emergencies. If you suspect either, get the person somewhere warm and contact emergency services or a medical professional. This guide is general information, not medical advice.

A simple checklist to set up before winter

  • Decide now which room will be your warm room.
  • Keep extra blankets, sleeping bags and wool socks where you can find them in the dark.
  • Store flashlights or lanterns and spare batteries.
  • Install battery-powered CO alarms and test them.
  • Get your fireplace or wood stove inspected if you plan to rely on it.
  • Know where your water shutoff is.
  • Keep some food that doesn't need cooking, and water stored for drinking.

Planning beyond one cold night

Keeping one room warm will get most families through a short outage safely.
